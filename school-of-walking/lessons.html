Lessons

The first course has six lessons. Each starts from footage, shows what the machine measured, gives the rule the machine would write, and ends with an exercise for your own feet. The samples are small: read the rules as observations, not laws.

Lesson 1

Rhythm

Every walk is a rhythm. A foot lands, the body falls forward, the other foot catches it. The machine cannot always see feet clearly, so it watches the distance between the ankles open and close, and counts each widest moment as a step.

On a winter crossing in Tyumen it drew six people step by step. The white figure is a foot striking the ground; the faint one is a leg in the air.

What the machine counted

One full cycle, two steps: 0.92 s.

Six walkers: 103 to 132 steps a minute.

Medians across the archive: Toronto (1928) 115, Munich 112, Tyumen 120.

The machine's rule

Land a foot every half second.

A median, turned into a norm. This is how machine rules are made; the school prints them so you can disagree.

Exercise

Walk one street with a metronome at 114 beats a minute, a foot on every beat. Switch it off at the corner and walk on. Count again after a minute: did the beat stay?

Ancestor

Wilhelm Braune and Otto Fischer, Der Gang des Menschen (1895): the first three-dimensional measurement of a human walk.

Lesson 2

Speed

Speed is tempo times stride. To walk faster you can step more often or step further. To see which one people change, the machine measured twenty-three passers-by in two cities, ninety years apart.

It measures in body heights, so that a person far from the camera counts the same as a person close to it.

What the machine counted

Toronto (1928): 115 steps a minute, stride 0.42 body heights.

Munich: 112 steps a minute, stride 0.42 body heights.

Share of the spread in speed that came from longer steps: 68% in Toronto, 43% in Munich.

The machine's rule

To go faster, take longer steps, not quicker ones.

True for Toronto in 1928. In Munich today, speed came about equally from both. The rule is already out of date.

Exercise

Walk one block at your usual pace and count the steps. Walk it again in a hurry and count again. If the second number is smaller, you lengthened your stride.

Ancestor

Robert Levine and Ara Norenzayan, The Pace of Life in 31 Countries (1999): they timed pedestrians over 60 feet of pavement in 31 cities.

Lesson 3

Counterflow

Sixty people walk round a ring corridor at INRIA in Rennes. Each was told one of two directions; nobody was told to form lanes.

The machine followed every path through the frame and measured, moment by moment, how many of each walker's neighbours were going the same way. A value of 1 would mean perfect lanes; 0.5 means mixed at random.

What the machine counted

110 tracks walking right, median 0.60 m/s.

79 tracks walking left, median 0.57 m/s.

Slower than 0.4 m/s: 27% of the time.

Lane order: 0.47 in the first five seconds, 0.54 after.

The machine's rule

Walk behind someone going your way.

Lanes formed a little without anyone asking. The machine turns a slight tendency into an instruction.

Exercise

In a crowded passage, follow one stranger who is walking your way for twenty metres. Then walk twenty metres against the flow. Count how many times you had to stop.

Ancestor

William H. Whyte, The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces (1980): he filmed New York plazas and found that people stop to talk right in the main flow.

Lesson 4

The room

Inside a café the machine stops counting steps and draws three other things: where each head points, the route each body takes across the floor, and each time a hand lifts a cup to a mouth.

It decides what counts as a look with a rule of its own. A glance that ends too soon did not happen.

What the machine counted

Look: a head ray, from the ears through the nose, lands on another head at a similar depth for at least 0.3 s.

Sip: a wrist stays within 1.25 head widths of the mouth for at least 0.2 s.

Route: the point on the floor under a body, a dot every 22 cm.

The machine's rule

A look shorter than 0.3 seconds did not happen.

People do most of their looking in glances shorter than that. The machine's rule erases them.

Exercise

Sit in a café for ten minutes. Count every time a stranger's glance meets yours. Then decide which of them were long enough to count.

Ancestor

Erving Goffman, Behavior in Public Places (1963): civil inattention, the brief look that shows you have seen someone and then turns away.

Lesson 5

Formation

A parade is walking with the individual taken out. In Germany in 1944 rows of men lift the same foot at the same time. Two years later, in an American restaurant, every body moves on its own clock.

The machine draws both films with the same marks: skeletons, head rays, rings where feet land. Watch the rings. In the parade they belong to a drill; in the restaurant they belong to nobody in particular.

What the machine counted

Nothing yet. The parade film is short and cut too often to time the steps of a whole row.

The machine's rule

No rule.

The school does not teach marching. A rule for it already exists, and this film shows where it leads.

Exercise

Walk beside a friend for five minutes without talking. Notice the moment your steps fall into time, and break it on purpose.

Ancestor

Marcel Mauss, Techniques of the Body (1934): in the First World War he watched an English regiment try to march to French bugles. Their gait was at odds with the music, and the regiment had to give up its French buglers. A walk is learned, and it is hard to unlearn.

Lesson 6

Cinema

Marcel Mauss noticed it in a New York hospital: his nurses walked like women in American films. Back in Paris, French girls had started to walk the same way.

Cinema was the first school of walking with a mass audience. The school's archive films, from a Toronto street in 1928 to a restaurant in 1946, are lessons nobody announced as lessons.

What the machine counted

Toronto (1928), 14 passers-by: median 115 steps a minute, stride 0.42 body heights.

Silent film was often cranked slower than 24 frames a second, so 1928 tempos may read high.

The machine's rule

Copy the walk you see most often.

This is how a machine learns: it repeats what is frequent. It is also how Mauss's nurses learned.

Exercise

Watch one minute of a film made before you were born, with the sound off. Then walk one block like the person who walks most in it.

Ancestor

Marcel Mauss, Techniques of the Body (1934), the passage on the American walk; and the walking plates of Eadweard Muybridge (1887) and Étienne-Jules Marey (1894).
